R1-Maj-3: MRI Comparison Is Not Formal Validation
Reviewer comment: Section 5.3 lines 331-343: I find it hard to agree that this serves as formal validation. Since the model parameters have been set the same for both sex and heart failure types, the simulated volumes are biased towards these 'generalized' parameters.

We thank the reviewer for this important point. We agree that the analyses presented do not constitute formal patient-specific validation and we have revised the manuscript accordingly. The purpose of this study is not to demonstrate subject-specific predictive accuracy under individually calibrated electrophysiological or mechanical parameters. Rather, it is to use a controlled in-silico framework to isolate how variation in anatomical geometry, across sex and heart failure groups, influences simulated outputs when all non-geometric model assumptions are held constant. For this reason, comparisons to UK Biobank reference ranges are now described as an anatomical fidelity check, not functional validation. They assess whether the reconstructed models reproduce plausible population-level anatomical distributions, but they do not establish that the simulations recover each patient’s true physiological state. We now state this limitation explicitly throughout the manuscript. 
To provide a limited physiological cross-check within the scope of the available data, we additionally examined the relationship between simulated ventricular total activation time and recorded QRS duration in the subset with ECG data. We show that ventricular total activation time (TAT) correlates with recorded ECG QRS duration (n=33 of 50 cases with available ECG data). In control hearts, TAT showed a positive correlation with QRS duration (Pearson r = 0.649, p = 0.005), suggesting that TAT captures part of the anatomical contribution to QRS duration. We interpret this analysis cautiously: it supports the view that anatomy contributes to inter-subject variation in activation timing, but it does not replace formal validation against matched patient-specific functional measurements. Full validation of patient-specific predictive performance would require richer case-level data, including gated imaging, electrophysiology, and calibration of tissue properties, which were not available for this retrospective cohort.

Changes made: Discussion Section 5.3 rewritten: 'validation' replaced with 'anatomical fidelity check'; explicit statement on uniform material property limitation added [pages 15-16, lines 396-410]. TAT-QRS correlation added to Results [page 13, lines 329-338] and Limitations [page 17, lines 489-496].

Response to Reviewer 2
Reviewer 2 raised five technical questions, all of which require specific numerical or methodological answers. Each is addressed below with the exact data from our pipeline. These were the highest-priority items in the revision.

R2-1: CT Acquisition Phase and Internal Pressure
Reviewer comment: Are the reconstructed meshes referred to the same timepoint of the cardiac cycle? What is the internal pressure at that instant?

We thank the reviewer for this question. All CT scans were acquired using a consistent imaging protocol across the cohort. The reconstructed meshes represent the cardiac anatomy in a static reference configuration. While the scans are not ECG-gated to a specific cardiac phase, the use of a uniform protocol ensures anatomical consistency for comparative analysis.
In these patients we do not have diastolic measurements. We are not trying to replicate a specific patient but to quantify the impact of anatomical variation across cohorts when consistent parameters are used. Our simulation framework applies prescribed endocardial pressures (7 mmHg for left chambers, 3.5 mmHg for right chambers) from this baseline geometric state, enabling direct comparison of how anatomical differences attributed to sex and disease influence mechanical response under identical loading conditions.

Changes made: Added clarification to Materials Section 2 regarding CT acquisition protocol and reference configuration [3, lines 69-73].

R2-2: Finite Element Type
Reviewer comment: Which type of finite elements are supported by the mesh-generator?

We thank the reviewer for this question. The CGAL mesh generator produces unstructured volumetric meshes composed of linear tetrahedral elements (4 nodes per element), with target element edge length set to approximately 0.5 mm. After all model processing steps (fibre assignment, coordinate mapping, and region tagging), the final simulation-ready models contained a mean of 2.6×10⁶ elements per heart (range 1.9–4.5×10⁶). Complete CGAL parameter specifications are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Changes made: Added element type specification and final mesh statistics to Methods Section 3.1.2 [pages 5-6, lines 117-132].

R2-3: Mesh Quality Assessment
Reviewer comment: Have the authors checked the quality of the generated meshes, for instance, by computing the determinant of the Jacobian of the geometrical map?

We thank the reviewer for this important question. We have now assessed mesh quality using the volume-based tetrahedral distortion metric (tet_qmetric_volume) implemented in meshtool, which is directly related to the Jacobian determinant of the geometric map. We included Figure S3 in the supplementary material, showing the distribution of the distortion metric across all meshes in the cohort and Supplementary Data S8, a CSV file with all the outputs from this analysis. 
Across all 50 meshes (mean 2.6×10⁶ elements per mesh), the mean element quality was 0.153 ± 0.100 on a normalised scale where 0 represents a perfect tetrahedron and 1 represents full degeneracy. The minimum quality per mesh averaged 2.1×10⁻⁴, indicating that severely distorted elements were rare. Critically, no inverted elements (quality > 0.99) were detected in any mesh, and near-degenerate elements (quality > 0.90) were found in only 28 of 50 meshes, comprising at most 9 elements per mesh (< 0.0003% of total elements).
All simulations converged without numerical instability, providing additional functional confirmation of mesh suitability.

Changes made: Mesh quality assessment added to Methods Section 3.1.2 [page 7, lines 171-182].

R2-4: Geometry-Image Correspondence and Model Alignment
Reviewer comment: Have the authors assessed the quality of the reconstructed geometry compared to the clinical images? How are the models aligned to perform and compare the simulations?

We thank the reviewer for this two-part question.
Regarding model alignment: each patient-specific mesh was simulated in its native coordinate system without spatial registration to a common anatomical reference frame. This approach is appropriate because all comparisons are performed on scalar outputs (chamber volumes, total activation times, volume changes) that are invariant to rigid spatial transformations. Universal Ventricular Coordinates (UVCs) provide normalised transmural and apicobasal coordinates for any regional functional analysis, enabling comparison independent of absolute spatial positioning. This has been clarified in Methods Section 3.3.
Regarding geometry-image correspondence: full voxel-level correspondence assessment of the 37-label post-processed geometry against clinical images is not feasible for this dataset for two reasons. First, CT soft-tissue contrast does not resolve myocardial boundaries outside the LV; structures including the RV wall, atria, and great vessel walls are assigned thickness via distance maps at a user-specified constant, and the SVC and IVC are not visible in standard clinical CT scans. Second, there is no independent ground-truth segmentation available. For the 10-label segmentation stage, geometric accuracy is supported by the published validation of the deep learning model (Xu et al.). Population-level consistency of chamber volumes with UKBB reference ranges (Section 5.3) provides indirect geometric plausibility evidence at the cohort level. These constraints are acknowledged as a limitation in Discussion Section 5.5.1.

Changes made: Spatial alignment clarification added to Methods Section 3.3 [page 9, lines 188-193]. Geometry-image correspondence limitation added to Discussion Section 5.5.1 [page 18, lines 516-523].

R2-5: User Control of Mesh Density
Reviewer comment: Do the users have the possibility of controlling and adapting the mesh by varying the density in specific (user-defined) regions?

We thank the reviewer for this question. Mesh density is controlled via CGAL parameters (facet_size, cell_size, facet_distance), which are specified globally for the entire heart geometry in the current implementation. While regional refinement is theoretically possible by meshing structures separately and stitching at interfaces, we prioritised maintaining mesh integrity and element quality consistency across the whole heart to ensure numerical stability in coupled electromechanical simulations, where interface discontinuities can introduce artefacts. Complete parameter specifications are provided in Supplementary Table S2. The trade-offs of regional refinement are discussed in the Workflow Insights subsection.

Changes made: Mesh control documentation added to Methods Section 3.1.2 [page 5, lines 119-125]. Discussion of regional refinement trade-offs added to Discussion Section 5.1 [page 15, lines 366-370].

R3-Maj-2 / R3-Maj-6: Lack of Proper Validation
Reviewer comment: The main issue with the paper is lack of proper validation. There is comparison of lung volumes and ephys between groups, but there is no validation back to patient data. Section 5.3 — given the point above, this was not a convincing argument.

We thank the reviewer for this comment. We agree that the original text overstated the extent to which the present analyses could be interpreted as validation against patient data, and we have revised the manuscript to correct this. In the revised version, we make a clearer distinction between two separate questions: whether the virtual cohort is anatomically plausible at the population level, and whether the simulations achieve patient-specific predictive accuracy. The present study addresses the former, but not the latter. Our objective here is to examine how differences in anatomical geometry across sex and heart failure groups affect simulated outputs under a common set of model assumptions, rather than to reproduce each patient’s physiology through case-specific calibration. Accordingly, the comparison to UK Biobank reference ranges (Section 5.3) is now described as an anatomical fidelity check rather than formal validation. We interpret that result conservatively: it indicates that anatomy contributes to observed inter-subject variation in activation timing, but it does not establish patient-specific validation.
